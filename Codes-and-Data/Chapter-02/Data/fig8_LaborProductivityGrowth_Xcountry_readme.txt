Figure 8: Labor productivity growth for a selection of countries, 1891-2023. 
Labor productivity is measured as GDP per hour worked. 
Sources: 
(a) For years 1891-2012: Bergeaud, A., Cette, G. and Lecat, R. (2016) "Productivity Trends in Advanced Countries between 1890 and 2012," Review of Income and Wealth. 
http://www.longtermproductivity.com/post.html 
Series (a) is reported as a labor productivity index, which I converted to a growth rate by taking the first difference of the log of the index and multiplying by 100. 
(b) For years 1891-2023: Labor productivity growth rate data is from OECD
OECD (2023), Labour productivity and utilisation (indicator). doi: 10.1787/02c02f63-en (Accessed on 27 December 2023)
https://data.oecd.org/lprdty/labour-productivity-and-utilisation.htm#indicator-chart 
Series (b) is reported as a labor productivity growth rate, which is already in percentage form. 
The full sample (1891-2023) is a combination of the two series (a) and (b). 
The data is Hodrick-Prescott filtered following Bergeaud et al (2016) using a lambda of 500.
Note: Bergeaud et al. (2016) states: 
"In order to establish the stylized facts of productivity growth, we smooth the
 annual productivity growth rate over the period using the Hodrick–Prescott filtration (HP).
Considering the very high volatility of our data, the choice of the
lambda coefficient, which sets the length of the cycle we capture, is of paramount
importance. Setting too high a value for lambda would tend to absorb smaller
cycles, while setting too low a value would result in major cyclical effects being
considered to be trends, especially around WWII. We decided to focus on 30-year
cycles, which implies a value of 500 for lambda, according to the HP filter transfer function."
